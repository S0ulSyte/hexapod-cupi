# Measure the motor, randomize the rest

Sim-to-real failures in legged locomotion concentrate in two unglamorous places — actuation-and-timing fidelity and deployment-pipeline parity — and the field's strongest transfers come from measuring a handful of plant parameters before reaching for domain randomization. The canonical catastrophes are precisely quantified: ANYmal policies trained against an ideal actuator model **"could not take a single step without falling"**, and a learned actuator network that cut torque-prediction error from **3.55 to 0.74 N·m** was the difference between zero steps and zero-shot transfer ([Hwangbo et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08652)); the Minitaur — the closest direct-drive precedent — transferred only once an analytic DC-motor model and a measured **15–19 ms** loop latency went into the simulator ([Tan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10332)); and a 32 kg hexapod that walked backward fine yet fell every time it walked forward was cured by randomizing a joint static friction **nine times larger** than quadruped norms ([Hu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01255v1)). Blanket randomization is not a substitute: it measurably cuts performance (top speed 1.1→0.9 m/s, mass tolerance halved in one controlled study ([Xie et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.02404))), so the consensus ladder is to identify latency, motor saturation and mass; randomize around measured uncertainty; let history-conditioned policies or learned estimators absorb the rest; buy smoothness with reward penalties; and treat observation assembly, export normalization and handover state as first-class engineering, because community post-mortems trace as many hardware failures to those as to physics. For an 18-joint direct-drive hexapod trained with PPO in Isaac Sim at 50 Hz position control, the survey's bottom line is encouraging — that is the modal published recipe, and direct drive deletes the gearbox and series-elastic modeling problems that forced actuator networks — but four obligations follow: reproduce the 0.040 rad / 20 ms rate limiter bit-exactly inside the training action path; widen Isaac Lab defaults, which ship with ground friction *unrandomized*; shape an explicitly tripod-style reward, since quadruped reward recipes demonstrably fail on six legs; and spend bench hours on three numbers — loop latency, torque-vs-voltage saturation, and joint friction.

## Actuators and timing, not contact physics, cause the famous failures

The best-documented transfer failures are actuator stories. ETH's ANYmal team built an analytical model of their series-elastic actuator — roughly **100 parameters, at least three weeks** to construct, more than a week of tuning — and it still could not produce a single step on hardware; the ideal-PD alternative mispredicted torque by **3.55 N·m mean (9–14% of the 40 N·m actuator range)** and produced violent limb shaking attributed to unmodeled delays. A small supervised MLP trained on **under four minutes** of logged excitation data (sine foot trajectories at 1–25 Hz, logged at 400 Hz) replaced it, cut error roughly fivefold, and enabled zero-shot locomotion and first-try fall recovery ([Hwangbo et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08652)). On the direct-drive side, Tan et al. found the stock PyBullet position constraint overdamped, replaced it with an ideal DC motor — torque constant and resistance from the datasheet, back-EMF, battery-voltage-scaled PWM, and a piecewise-linear torque-current saturation lookup — and measured latency with a one-timestep command spike: **3 ms** in the servo microcontroller, **15–19 ms** through the Jetson. Removing either the motor model or the latency model broke transfer outright ([Tan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10332)). The cost persists even on well-engineered platforms: Mini Cheetah reached **3.81 m/s real with system identification versus 2.49 m/s without** (sim claimed ~5 m/s either way) ([Margolis et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02824)), and the RAI Institute's Spot work found its pre-identification policy simply out of distribution on hardware — flight-phase gaits only worked after CMA-ES fitting of torque-speed curves and per-joint friction, which turned out to vary by a factor of ~20 between lead-screw knees (0.180 N·m) and hips (0.008 N·m) ([Miller et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17857)).

Two findings in this literature matter disproportionately for a position-controlled robot. First, DeepMind's OP3 soccer team diagnosed *why* position control transfers deceptively well and then fails: tracking error silently absorbs dynamics mismatch until it cannot, and their agents trained without randomization and perturbations fell every one or two steps ([Haarnoja et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13653)). Position mode hides the gap; it does not close it. Second, measured end-to-end latencies cluster around one 50 Hz control period — 15–19 ms on Minitaur, **~20 ms on the Go1**, modeled there as a constant one-step action delay ([Margolis & Agrawal 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03238)) — and latency is the single parameter with the strongest ablation evidence anywhere in the field. In the canonical dynamics-randomization ablation, fixing the action timestep collapsed real-robot score from **0.89 to 0.29** ([Peng et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06537)); a vision quadruped without delay randomization covered roughly half the course distance of its delay-randomized twin (444.7 vs 859.9) ([Imai et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.14549)); and policies trained without latency randomization failed beyond 17 ms of actual latency, while training over 0–20 ms extended tolerance to 32 ms ([Xie et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.02404)). At club scale, a Mini Pupper 2 study measured over 50 ms of transport delay and trained robustness out to +320 ms ([Weddington et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26434)).

Power and heat are the slow-motion versions of the same failure. Tan randomized battery voltage over 14.0–16.8 V; the SpiderPi hexapod's real-world failure rates rose when its pack sagged to **~10 V from a nominal 12 V** ([Qu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10628)); OP3 behavior varied strongly with charge, limiting runs to 5–10 minutes ([Haarnoja et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13653)); ToddlerBot's walking endurance capped at **19 minutes** because rising motor temperature pushed dynamics out of the training distribution ([Shi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00893)); and putting motor temperature into the observation with a thermal penalty extended an A1's loaded march from ~7 minutes (knee overheat shutdown) to over 27, battery-limited ([Qian et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01631)). Contact physics, by contrast, produces subtler and two-sided errors: simulators reproduce inelastic impacts well but elastic ones poorly ([Acosta et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00541)); friction coefficients are engine artifacts — NVIDIA's own docs warn PhysX patch friction feels grippier than per-contact friction at the same coefficient ([Isaac Lab solver comparison](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v3.0.0-beta2/source/overview/core-concepts/physical-backends/solver-comparison.html)); policies can exploit sim contact models with hard impacts that underperform on hardware ([Bogdanovic et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.06629)); and the gap cuts both ways — one loco-manipulation team saw persistent yaw oscillation in Isaac Gym that never appeared on the real robot ([Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18938)).

## The second gap: deployment plumbing fails as often as dynamics

A striking fraction of documented hardware failures have nothing to do with the simulator's physics. The longest-running open issue in Unitree's own RL deployment repo — robots that shake in place for a few seconds and fall, running the vendor's reference pipeline — carries an in-thread diagnosis checklist that puts wrong angular-velocity frame (world vs body), wrong joint order, and wrong observation scaling first, followed by PD gains, default-pose offsets and handover state silently diverging between the training config and the deploy config ([unitree_rl_gym issue #26](https://github.com/unitreerobotics/unitree_rl_gym/issues/26)). The same class is baked into tooling: Isaac Lab's policy exporter takes the observation normalizer as an optional argument and bakes in an Identity module when it is omitted, so a policy trained with empirical normalization silently receives raw observations on the robot ([Isaac Lab rsl_rl exporter](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab)). Menlo's humanoid deployment guide records a leg-oscillation incident with two simultaneous causes — underdamped gains and out-of-order CAN responses that made the policy treat stale state as real deviation — where fixing either alone was insufficient and reward tweaks fixed nothing; their stack now validates against an actuator emulator with 0.4–2 ms injected response jitter before any assembled robot walks ([Menlo deployment guide](https://docs.menlo.ai/guides/locomotion-training/reinforcement-learning-policy-deployment)). The lesson these sources converge on: when a transferred policy shakes while standing, audit observation assembly, timing and handover pose before touching the policy or rewards — the recommended audit being to log ~500 steps of raw robot state and diff every observation term against a sim rebuild ([unitree_rl_gym issue #26](https://github.com/unitreerobotics/unitree_rl_gym/issues/26)).

Deployment practice has correspondingly standardized into a staged ladder. Teams replay the policy in a second simulator first — Isaac-trained policies into MuJoCo, Cassie policies into MATLAB SimMechanics, Digit policies through Agility's simulator to filter unsafe candidates ([Radosavovic et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03381)) — while noting that Isaac Gym and MuJoCo contact models diverge enough to change behavior ([Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18938)) and that sim-to-sim success does not guarantee sim-to-real success ([CPG-RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00458)). Then comes a bench or hoist stage, then floor trials with a documented kill path. The deploy-side safety layer has a published generic spec: schema check rejecting NaN/Inf, joint-range clamp, per-step delta clamp, slew-rate limit, a watchdog that holds a safe target when no fresh action arrives, a roll/pitch envelope fault, and an operator stop that cuts torque regardless of state — with acceptance tests that inject a NaN, inject an out-of-range target, and cut the policy feed mid-run ([UniLab safety layers](https://unilabsim.github.io/UniLab-doc)). Universal companions are vendor torque protection (Walk These Ways runs Unitree PowerProtect at level 9), soft-start interpolation to the default pose before the policy takes over — handing control from a pose the policy never saw is a ranked failure cause — and missed-deadline awareness: OP3's 40 Hz agent often failed to produce an action within its 25 ms window on a dual-core CPU, which the authors list among performance limits ([Haarnoja et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13653)). Small-MLP inference itself is never the bottleneck; measured costs run from 25 µs (ANYmal) to 10 µs (Solo12's Raspberry Pi 4) ([Aractingi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16683)).

## Identify first, randomize to measured uncertainty, let history absorb the rest

The evidence ranks system identification above randomization. Beyond the Minitaur, Mini Cheetah and Spot results above, sampling-based identification on Go2/G1 (CMA-ES over a multi-step state-prediction cost) recovered base mass, CoM, inertia and per-motor torque-saturation gains from **about one minute** of data and cut task error 42–63% — while in the same benchmark a "Heavy DR" policy scored *worse than the uncalibrated simulator* on jump tasks (normalized error 1.75 and 1.40 vs 1.0) ([Pei et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14266)). Controlled studies agree: with a velocity estimator and soft gains, Laikago policies transferred with *no* dynamics randomization at all, and adding DR cut trotting top speed from 1.1 to 0.9 m/s and halved payload tolerance, with latency the lone exception that genuinely repaid randomizing ([Xie et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.02404)); Tan found randomization bought robustness at the price of conservative, lower-return gaits ([Tan et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10332)); and a practitioner synthesis from the Spot/UT Austin orbit advises using less domain randomization than one expects, with principled system identification as the single most critical step ([Morgenstein 2025](https://thehumanoid.ai/deployment-ready-rl-pitfalls-lessons-and-best-practices/)). What system ID costs, the literature prices generously low for this robot class: weigh the links (masses become trustworthy; CAD inertial error ran to ~20% on ANYmal from unmodeled cabling ([Hwangbo et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08652))), spike-test latency at both the servo and policy tiers, and — for position-mode servos specifically — run a pendulum-bench identification of the firmware control law plus extended friction, which cut model error 2–3x versus the Coulomb-viscous friction that MuJoCo and Isaac Gym implement by default ([Duclusaud et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08650)). Learned actuator networks remain the tool for actuators with hidden internal state (springs, high gearing, opaque firmware): Isaac Lab ships them as first-class `ActuatorNetMLP/LSTM` classes ([Isaac Lab actuator docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/concepts/actuators.html)), but a direct-drive platform should try the analytic route first — that is exactly how the Minitaur transferred.

Randomization then covers the measured residual, and its canon is stable enough to tabulate. The de-facto standard set descends from legged_gym into Isaac Lab and the Unitree forks, and the hardware-focused papers add actuation and timing terms:

The ablation ordering within that table is worth internalizing: latency/timing first (0.89→0.29 in the Peng ablation), observation noise second (0.89→0.25), friction and mass after that ([Peng et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.06537)). And randomization only pays when the policy can exploit it — memory is the enabling mechanism, with the LSTM scoring 0.89 on hardware against 0.67 for feedforward in the same study, and recurrent policies repeatedly succeeding where feedforward ones fail on stairs ([Siekmann et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.08328)). The structured extensions all follow that logic: teacher-student distillation gives a privileged teacher (true heights, contacts, friction) and distills into a proprioceptive-history student, with zero failures over four 60-minute DARPA SubT missions as the flagship result ([Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251)); RMA regresses a dynamics latent from 0.5 s of state history and runs it asynchronously at 10 Hz ([Kumar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034)); concurrent estimators train a velocity/contact head inside the PPO loop ([Ji et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05481)), and DreamWaQ collapses the whole two-stage pipeline into one asymmetric actor-critic stage with 95% 30-minute survival on hardware ([Nahrendra et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.10602)). When a gap persists after deployment, the modern residual fix is action-space correction learned from real rollouts — a delta-action model as in ASAP ([He et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01143)), or simulator adaptation by matching proprioceptive rollout *distributions* from under five minutes of untimed hardware data ([Dao & Fern 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11090)) — a lineage that began with grounded action transformation improving a NAO's walk speed 43% ([Hanna & Stone 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/11124)).

Reward-side smoothness is the remaining universal ingredient. Every deployed stack carries the same penalty family — action rate (~−0.01), joint acceleration (~−2.5e-7), torque (~−1e-5 to −2e-5), foot slip — with coefficients remarkably stable across 10–50 kg robots ([legged_gym config](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym/blob/master/legged_gym/envs/base/legged_robot_config.py)), annealed in by a curriculum (starting at ×0.03–0.3 of final strength) so they do not prevent walking from ever emerging ([Kumar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034); [Hwangbo et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08652)). The cleanest hardware quantification of what smoothness buys comes from a quadrotor: condition-action-policy smoothness regularization cut motor current from 22.87 to 4.86 A and the oscillation metric by ~96% ([CAPS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.06644)) — the cheapest insurance against direct-drive chatter available, at the cost of some tracking sharpness.

## Six legs demand gait-shaped rewards and buy cheap failure, not free transfer

The hexapod-specific literature is small, recent, and converging on the same pipeline this project runs: its strongest results — SJTU's 25.5 kg motion-priors hexapod, Lenovo's Saturn Lite, Berkeley's SpiderPi line, FZI's LAURON VI — are all Isaac Gym/legged_gym-family systems with PPO or RMA-style distillation. Its most load-bearing single lesson is a reward lesson: **reward formulations borrowed from quadrupeds failed to produce tripod gaits**, and SJTU had to build a dedicated tripod-style adversarial-motion-prior reward from trajectory-optimized flat-ground gaits; removing it brought jitter, velocity-tracking error and stair failures ([Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03167)). LAURON VI likewise carries an explicit symmetric-tripod reward term — and its RL controller, running a per-joint actuator network plus a low-pass filter on joint commands, was the fastest of three controller families tested on the same 42 kg hardware, at 0.43 m/s versus 0.17 for the kinematic engine and 0.10 for MPC ([FZI 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07689v1)). No successful hexapod RL-on-hardware result in this survey reports a clean tripod gait emerging from a plain velocity-tracking reward; explicit gait shaping (AMP prior, symmetry term, or CPG scaffold) should be treated as near-mandatory. Gait *clocks*, by contrast, transfer cleanly — they are internal counters, not sensors — and the deployment-proven patterns are per-leg sinusoidal phase inputs with inter-leg offsets and a stand-still state machine that freezes the phase at zero command ([Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251); [Margolis & Agrawal 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03238)).

The second hexapod lesson is actuator friction, and it is dramatic: Saturn Lite's policies passed sim-to-sim checks, walked backward on hardware without issue, and fell whenever they walked forward. The team identified joint static friction at **0.442 N·m — about nine times the Unitree Go1's 0.0481** — caused by waterproof sealing rings and heavy links, and found that inserting the exact identified value into training stalled learning while iterative fine-tuning produced jitter; what worked was deliberately widening the randomization to a 0.0–1.2 multiplier range, after which the robot walked forward stably and climbed stairs ([Hu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01255v1)). That resolves the identify-versus-randomize tension into a precise recipe: measure to learn the *scale* of a parameter, then randomize a band around it rather than pinning it. Third, the platform's static stability is a real but bounded asset. Sources credit hexapods with a support tripod at all times, which shows up as benign exploration and recoverable falls rather than automatic transfer — no quantitative hexapod-versus-quadruped transfer benchmark exists — and it enables a distinctive fallback no quadruped enjoys so cheaply: learning or adapting directly on hardware. The Nature damage-recovery hexapod precomputed ~13,000 gaits in simulation with MAP-Elites and then adapted on the physical robot in typically fewer than 10 trials and about a minute or two, across six damage types including two missing legs ([Cully et al. 2015](https://members.loria.fr/JBMouret/nature_press.html)), and the HEBI Daisy hexapod learned to walk in under 10 hardware trials via simulation-informed Bayesian optimization ([Rai et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.04796)).

The club and hobbyist record, finally, is mostly electrical rather than algorithmic. Documented learned-policy deployments on hobby hexapods are rare — Berkeley's teacher-student policies on the $600, 18-joint SpiderPi (stair-climb success up to 7.0/7.0 flights, squeezing 85–100%) stand as the existence proof that distillation tolerates bus servos with **no real-time joint feedback at all** ([Qu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10628); [Kawawa-Beaudan 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-146.html)) — while the recurring community failures are power-supply sag (budget for ~2.5 A stall per MG996R-class servo ([Arduino forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/power-requirements-for-18-mg996r-servos/1157351))), servos held near stall by the standing posture overheating ([Arduino forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/robotic-arm-servos-overheating/524454)), reliable travel closer to ~120° with unit-to-unit variance, so commanded ranges must stay away from endstops ([Parallax forums](https://forums.parallax.com/discussion/comment/1572495)), and outright counterfeit servos ([r/robotics](https://redlib.hackliberty.org/r/robotics/comments/1hheckn/hexapod_project_update)). Nobody at club scale reports a raw joint-level PPO policy transferred blind onto PWM servos and working; a feedback-capable direct-drive 18-joint build sits above that hardware class, in the SJTU/Lenovo/LAURON regime where friction identification, measured-uncertainty randomization and tripod-shaped rewards are the documented recipe.

## For this rig: train through the limiter, fix Isaac defaults, measure three numbers

The target configuration — 50 Hz position targets over a fast onboard PD loop — is the modal published recipe of 2021–2026, shared by Mini Cheetah, Go1, ANYmal-C's perceptive controller, Digit and the SJTU hexapod, and the two closest platform analogs both transferred zero-shot: the 18-joint hexapod at 50 Hz with action offsets on a nominal stance and smoothness enforced by reward alone ([Liu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03167)), and the direct-drive-class Solo12 at 100 Hz with no filter and no rate limit, whose authors attribute first-try transfer to lightweight, low-inertia, high-bandwidth actuators — the property a direct-drive hexapod shares ([Aractingi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16683)). Direct drive removes the two failure classes that dominated ANYmal's transfer (gearbox stiction and hidden elastic-actuator state), leaving back-EMF torque-speed limits, current saturation, supply sag, stiction and latency. Notably, the 0.040 rad / 20 ms limiter implies a 2.0 rad/s joint-velocity cap — far below the speeds where quadruped torque-speed corners bind (ANYmal joints reach 12 rad/s ([Hwangbo et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08652))) — so the binding actuator gaps here are stall-region torque (stiction plus voltage sag plus current limits) and timing, not the motor's speed envelope. ANYmal's ideal-model error of 9–14% of full-scale torque was enough to prevent a single step; a proportional error on a small direct-drive motor is plausible from stiction and sag alone, so small scale is not protective.

The rate limiter deserves singular attention because it is part of the plant. A hard slew limit in the deployed action path is exactly the UniLab delta-clamp/slew-rate safety layer ([UniLab safety layers](https://unilabsim.github.io/UniLab-doc)), and the documented mismatch risk is one-sided: a limiter present at deployment but absent (or different) in training changes the effective dynamics the policy learned against, the same mechanism as the Go1's non-ideal commanded-to-realized-position mapping that Walk These Ways trained an actuator network to capture ([Margolis & Agrawal 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03238)), compounded by position control's tendency to mask mismatch until it saturates ([Haarnoja et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13653)) and by Menlo's finding that logically valid commands with training-divergent timing still break the loop ([Menlo deployment guide](https://docs.menlo.ai/guides/locomotion-training/reinforcement-learning-policy-deployment)). The limiter must therefore be reproduced bit-exactly in the training action path — same clamp, same 20 ms cadence — and kept identical in every evaluator (the project's comparisons at matched achieved motion under the recorded limiter are the right instinct; the literature's analog is comparing loads at matched locomotion, as Hwangbo reports torque and power at matched gait). Pair it with the reward-side smoothness stack (first- and second-order action penalties at Go1-like weights) so the policy does not learn to saturate the limiter as a free low-pass filter, and keep PD gains low — kp=20, kd=0.5 at 50 Hz appears independently on Mini Cheetah and Go1 as a deliberately smoothness-first choice that also softens any sim-PD-versus-firmware-PD mismatch ([Margolis et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02824)).

On the Isaac side, four defaults need deliberate overrides. First, Isaac Lab's base velocity environment ships with **ground friction fixed** (static 0.8, dynamic 0.6) and the external-force hook zeroed — only mass scaling and modest pushes are on — so legged_gym-era robustness requires explicitly widening `physics_material` and enabling disturbances ([Isaac Lab velocity env](https://github.com/isaac-sim/IsaacLab/blob/main/source/isaaclab_tasks/isaaclab_tasks/core/velocity/velocity_env_cfg.py)). Second, implicit (solver-internal) and explicit (Python-side) PD actuators can respond differently with identical gains, and the actuator-model ladder — IdealPD, DCMotor with a torque-speed envelope, DelayedPD with a per-reset uniform delay buffer, ActuatorNet — is the sanctioned way to encode the motor model and measured latency; the docs' own guidance is to choose the simplest model that meets requirements ([Isaac Lab actuator docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/concepts/actuators.html)). Third, the standard solver configuration (TGS, 4 position / 0 velocity iterations, dt 5 ms, decimation 4) is tuned for quadrupeds; an 18-joint hexapod carries more simultaneous contacts, and Isaac Lab's contact-rich baseline raises position iterations to 8 with the documented symptom map that penetration and joint drift call for more position iterations first ([Isaac Lab solver tuning](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/how-to/solver_tuning_physx.html)). Fourth — directly relevant to recording ground-contact force during evaluations — Isaac Gym's reported net contact forces are **unreliable on GPU with trimesh terrain** (the legged_gym known-issue workaround is foot-only force sensors ([legged_gym](https://github.com/leggedrobotics/legged_gym))), and enabling PhysX stabilization can corrupt reported contact-sensor magnitudes ([Isaac Lab solver tuning](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/how-to/solver_tuning_physx.html)); contact-force instrumentation should be validated on plane or heightfield terrain before its numbers are trusted. On the export path, pass the normalizer explicitly or verify none was used ([Isaac Lab rsl_rl exporter](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab)).

The observation list needs the standard diet. Exact base linear velocity is the single most fragile sim-only input — every deployed system either estimates it (model-based filter, or a concurrent learned estimator that trains inside the PPO loop and whose removal caused the largest reward drop in ablation ([Ji et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05481))) or drops it entirely, as RMA does ([Kumar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034)); six legs give more stance contacts and thus better-conditioned kinematic velocity estimation, but slip still argues for training against estimator-realistic noise. Perfect terrain heights belong only on a teacher or critic; a deployable heightmap is a 20 Hz estimate with correlated artifacts — pose-drift offsets, occlusion holes — which Miki et al.'s three-regime noise model (60% nominal / 30% offset / 10% mapping-failure) randomizes explicitly, and which plain white noise on height samples does not represent ([Miki et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08117)). Finally, validation has a documented ladder that matches instrumented evaluation: per-joint sine/chirp tracking on the bench, short open-loop replay of held-out trajectories (~1.5 s clips, beyond which contact chaos dominates ([Pei et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14266))), distributional comparison for longer horizons ([Dao & Fern 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11090)), then closed-loop tracking error, torque and power at matched motion with trial counts — the exact reporting shape of the ANYmal zero-shot results (0.143 m/s tracking error, 8.23 vs 11.7 N·m average torque against the model-based baseline ([Hwangbo et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08652))). Recording motor torque and ground-contact force during evaluations is precisely the instrumentation the literature used to close its gaps: the actuator network needed measured torque, and Spot's identification was scored against measured joint trajectories ([Miller et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17857)).

## Conclusion

Surveyed end to end, "the sim-to-real gap" resolves into three separable gaps with different owners: a plant-model gap (actuation, timing, power), an information gap (what the policy observes in sim that hardware cannot supply), and a parity gap (whether deployment reproduces training bit for bit). The literature's famous fixes address the first, its architectures the second — but the community record suggests the third claims the most first-deployment casualties, and it is also the cheapest to close, since it needs diffing tools and checklists rather than research. For a direct-drive hexapod the plant-model gap is unusually tractable — a datasheet motor law, one latency spike test, and a friction band learned from a bench are what transferred the Minitaur — which shifts the expected failure mass onto parity (where the 0.040 rad / 20 ms limiter is the single highest-leverage item: one clamp, reproduced exactly, or an entire plant nonlinearity goes unmodeled) and onto the one place hexapods genuinely differ from the quadruped canon, the gait prior.

Two things the field does not yet know are worth carrying forward. There is no controlled hexapod-versus-quadruped transfer benchmark, no ablation isolating foot collision geometry or smoothness penalties for transfer, and no published latency data for long daisy-chained servo buses — so a project that measures its own 18-servo bus latency distribution and logs per-joint torque against sim predictions is generating evidence the literature lacks, not merely consuming it. And the Saturn Lite result reframes the identify-versus-randomize debate in its most useful form: identification tells you where the truth lives, randomization admits you only know its neighborhood — the one documented hexapod-specific transfer cure was doing both, in that order.
